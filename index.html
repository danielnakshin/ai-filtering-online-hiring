Can an automated hiring system reject a qualified person before a human sees their application?

This website looks at applicant tracking systems used by job sites and employers, including platforms such as Workday and Greenhouse. It explains how automated filtering can make hiring faster while also creating or worsening discrimination based on race, gender, age, disability, immigration status, and other factors.

What is the problem?

An applicant tracking system, usually called an ATS, is software that helps employers collect and organize job applications. It can store resumes, post job openings, track candidates through interviews, and keep notes in one place. These functions can be useful when an employer receives hundreds or thousands of applications. An ATS makes it possible for a recruiting team to search records and follow the same basic workflow for each applicant. [5]

However, some systems do more than organize information. They may parse resumes into data fields, search for keywords, ask knockout questions, score assessments, rank applicants, or recommend which candidates should move forward. A person can therefore be screened out before a recruiter reads the full application. The system may misunderstand an unusual resume layout, treat a career gap as a negative sign, or favor the language used by applicants who already know how to write for automated filters. Commentary about ATS bias has described how apparently neutral rules can cause strong candidates to be overlooked. [3]

1. Application

A candidate uploads a resume and answers questions.

2. Parsing

The software turns the application into searchable fields.

3. Screening

Rules, scores, or models may decide who moves forward.

4. Human review

A recruiter may see only the applicants who remain.

History of applicant tracking systems

The first applicant tracking systems appeared in the 1970s. They were mainly designed to help employers manage records and applications, so most evaluation was still completed by people. During the 1980s, databases and more advanced search tools made it faster for recruiters to sort larger amounts of information. When online job boards became common in the 1990s and 2000s, applications could come from a much wider area. ATS platforms became connected to online job postings and became a central part of the hiring process. [1][2]

Modern systems offer more features. They can publish openings, identify possible candidates, parse resumes, send messages, schedule interviews, and automate repetitive tasks. Some tools also use data analytics or artificial intelligence to score or rank people. These features can increase productivity, but they also increase the power of the system. If an automated rule is unfair or unrelated to the job, it can affect many applicants quickly and quietly. The change from a record-keeping tool to a decision-influencing tool is why employers need to examine how each part of the system works.

Who is affected?

Anyone who applies for a job through an online system can be affected, but the risks are not distributed equally. Technology can repeat patterns from past hiring data or create new barriers through the design of an application, assessment, or ranking system. The EEOC and Department of Labor have identified concerns involving race, sex, disability, age, the digital divide, targeted advertising, and datasets that do not represent all groups fairly. [9]

Race and gender

A system does not need a field labeled “race” or “gender” to reproduce discrimination. Names, schools, ZIP codes, work history, and past hiring decisions can act as proxies. If a model learns from a workplace that previously favored one group, it may treat that pattern as the definition of a successful candidate. The result can look objective even though it was built from unequal conditions.

Age

Graduation dates, years of experience, salary history, and other information may correlate with age. In 2023, the EEOC announced a $365,000 settlement with iTutorGroup. The agency alleged that the company’s software automatically rejected women age 55 or older and men age 60 or older. More than 200 qualified applicants were allegedly screened out because of age. [4]

Disability

Timed tests, speech analysis, video interviews, mouse-dependent activities, and inaccessible forms can measure a disability or a person’s ability to use an interface instead of their ability to do the job. Federal guidance says employers should provide reasonable accommodations and should make sure a tool does not screen out a person who could do the work with or without an accommodation. [6]

Immigration status and language

Non-U.S. credentials, multilingual writing, name formats, employment authorization questions, and gaps caused by migration may be misunderstood by rigid systems. These problems can overlap with race, nationality, gender, disability, or lack of reliable internet access. Looking at these overlaps is called intersectionality. It helps explain why two people may experience the same application system very differently.

What has been done so far?

Existing U.S. employment laws already prohibit discrimination in selection procedures. The EEOC explains that tests and other hiring procedures may violate federal law if they intentionally discriminate or if they disproportionately exclude a protected group without a valid job-related reason. An employer is still responsible for the result even when the employer buys a tool from another company. [5]

The EEOC has also issued guidance about the Americans with Disabilities Act and the use of algorithms or artificial intelligence in employment. The guidance recommends a process for reasonable accommodations and warns that automated tools may screen out people with disabilities who could perform the job. [6]

Some local governments have created more specific rules. New York City’s Local Law 144 requires certain automated employment decision tools to receive a bias audit within one year of use. Employers also have to make information about the audit public and provide notices to affected candidates or employees. Enforcement began in July 2023. [7]

Advocacy groups, researchers, worker organizations, and government agencies have also asked companies to improve transparency, accessibility, auditing, and accountability. These actions are useful, but the issue is not fixed. A bias audit can examine only a narrow set of groups. A notice may not explain how a score affected the decision. A human reviewer may simply approve the system’s recommendation. Rules also differ depending on location, so many applicants do not receive the same protections.

Possible solutions

  1. Test outcomes regularly. Employers should compare selection rates at every stage, investigate large differences, and repeat the test after a tool is updated. An audit should ask whether the tool predicts something that actually matters for the job.
  2. Make applications accessible. Forms and tests should work with keyboards and screen readers. Employers should avoid unnecessary time limits and clearly explain how to request an accommodation or a non-automated alternative.
  3. Keep humans responsible. A reviewer should have enough time and authority to question a score. Having a person “in the loop” does not help if that person automatically accepts every recommendation.
  4. Give applicants notice and a way to appeal. People should know when an automated tool materially affects a decision, what kind of information it uses, and how to request a correction or reconsideration.
  5. Use less data. A hiring system should collect only information that is necessary and related to the job. This can reduce privacy risks and limit the number of irrelevant proxies that affect a decision.

Trade-offs and costs

Fairer systems take time and money. Independent audits, accessible alternatives, documentation, and appeals cost more than accepting a vendor score without review. Smaller employers may need shared standards or affordable outside support. Collecting demographic data can help reveal unfair outcomes, but it also creates privacy and security responsibilities. Human review can notice context that a model misses, but people can also be biased. The goal is not to assume that either humans or computers are always fair. The better approach is to use checks at every stage and make a real person or organization responsible for the final decision.
