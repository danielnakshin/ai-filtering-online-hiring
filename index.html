AI filtering and hiring

AI Filtering on Job Sites

This website looks at AI filtering on job sites such as Workday and Greenhouse. The goal is to understand whether people are being rejected from jobs because of discriminatory factors such as race, gender, age, and other information connected to their applications.

What is the problem?

An Applicant Tracking System, or ATS, provides the structure that allows recruitment to work at a large scale. It centralizes candidate information, job postings, applications, and hiring workflows into one system. [1]

Applicant tracking systems are often marketed as neutral, but technology is not neutral. A system can be affected by the data it was trained on and may reject qualified candidates before a human being ever sees their application. This can cause employers to overlook people who could bring real value to an organization simply because they did not use the “right” keywords on their resume. [3]

History of applicant tracking systems

The first applicant tracking systems were introduced in the 1970s. They were designed to manage applications, and most of the work was still done by people. During the 1980s, these systems began using more advanced filtering, which shortened the time recruiters needed to sort and analyze large numbers of resumes. As the internet grew, applicant tracking systems became connected with online jobs and made it faster for recruiters to sort through applications. [1][2]

Today’s applicant tracking systems are much more advanced. They can increase productivity, identify possible candidates, and automate repetitive tasks. These abilities make hiring faster, but they also allow the technology to have more influence over who gets considered for a job.

Who is affected?

Qualified applicants can be rejected when a system relies on flawed or biased data. Information connected to resume gaps, race, gender, ZIP codes, age, and other parts of an application can affect how a person is screened. The result can be discrimination against marginalized groups and a wider equality gap. [3]

This is also a risk for employers. In a competitive job market, a company can lose possible long-term employees because an applicant did not use the keywords that the system expected. The person may be rejected even though they are qualified for the role.

One example involved iTutorGroup. The company agreed to pay $365,000 to settle a lawsuit brought by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The EEOC said the company’s software automatically rejected women who were 55 or older and men who were 60 or older. [4]
