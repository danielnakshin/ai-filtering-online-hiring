Applicant Tracking Systems and Bias in Hiring

Applicant Tracking Systems are mostly used by employers to organize and filter job applications. These systems can make hiring decisions quicker and more efficient, but they can also prevent qualified people from reaching human recruiters. This project looks at how ATS technology works, how it can result in unfair hiring practices, who may be affected, and what can be done to make automated hiring fairer and more accessible to everyone.

Problem: How Applicant Tracking Systems Work

Applicant Tracking Systems, or ATS, are software platforms that employers use to collect, organize, search, and manage job applications. For example, when someone applies for a job online, their application may first enter an ATS instead of going directly to a human recruiter. The system can organize applicants based on information such as experience, education, skills, keywords, or answers to screening questions. Employers also use these systems because large companies can receive thousands of applications and need a quick and efficient way to filter them and narrow down the number of qualifying applicants.

ATS use is extremely common among large companies. In 2025, Jobscan found that 97.8% of Fortune 500 companies had this technology in their hiring process. [1] Harvard Business School research also found that about 63% of employers surveyed in the United States, United Kingdom, and Germany used a recruitment management system. Usage increased to 69% among employers with more than 1,000 workers. [2]

The main concern is that this technology can remove applicants before a recruiter personally reviews them. Harvard researchers found that among employers using recruitment management systems, 94% used them to rank or screen middle-skill applicants, while 92% did the same for high-skill applicants. [2] This gives an automated system a major role in deciding which resumes reach the next stage.

Although these tools help employers handle large applicant pools, very strict filters can also exclude qualified people whose resumes do not exactly match the filter. [2]

History of Applicant Tracking Systems

The first Applicant Tracking Systems were introduced in the 1970s. They were designed to manually manage applications, so most of the work was still done by humans. An Applicant Tracking System provides the structure that allows recruitment to function at scale by centralizing candidate information, job postings, applications, and hiring workflows into a single system. [3]

During the 1980s, Applicant Tracking Systems began incorporating more advanced filtering, shortening the time recruiters needed to sort and analyze large volumes of resumes. During the rise of the internet, Applicant Tracking Systems became integrated with online jobs, making it much faster for recruiters to sort through applications. [3][4]

Today’s Applicant Tracking Systems are far more advanced and offer more capabilities for organizations. They can increase productivity, identify passive candidates, and automate repetitive tasks. A 2025 McKinsey report showed that 80% of organizations had already adopted this technology in one or more areas of their operations.

Who Is Affected?

Applicant Tracking Systems are often marketed as neutral, but technology is never neutral. These systems can be unintentionally biased because of compromised or flawed training data, resulting in qualified candidates being rejected from roles they are qualified for. These individuals could bring real value to their chosen industry or organization but may be discarded without ever being seen by a human being.

This is not just a matter of unfairness; it is also a critical business risk. In a highly competitive talent market, companies cannot afford to lose potential long-term employees simply because they did not use the “right” keywords on their resumes. [5] These systems may rely on flawed data connected to resume gaps, race, gender, ZIP codes, age, and many other factors.

For example, iTutorGroup agreed to pay $365,000 to settle a lawsuit brought by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The company’s Applicant Tracking System automatically rejected women who were 55 or older and men who were 60 or older. [6] This is one of the costs of organizations using technology that discriminates against marginalized groups, widens the equality gap, and further disenfranchises people in the process.

What Has Been Done So Far?

As more people become aware of bias in Applicant Tracking Systems and other automated hiring tools, different groups have taken different approaches to combat the issue. One of the biggest changes has been increased government involvement. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, or EEOC, has made it clear that companies are still responsible for discrimination even when an algorithm or AI system is involved in hiring decisions. If a system unfairly rewards certain groups based on race, gender, religion, or other protected characteristics, the employer can still be held accountable. [7]

The Department of Justice and the EEOC have also explained that these systems can affect people with disabilities. Some tools may measure traits or behaviors that are not connected to a person’s ability to do the job, which can cause qualified applicants to be rejected for reasons that do not matter to the position. [8]

Some states and cities have begun creating laws specifically for automated hiring. New York City’s Local Law 144 requires certain automated employment decision tools to go through a bias audit before they are used, and employers must tell applicants when one of these systems will be used in the hiring process. [9] Illinois has also passed a law requiring employers that use AI to analyze video interviews to notify applicants, explain how the technology is being used, and receive the applicant’s consent before using the tool. [10]

Advocacy groups have also become involved in discussions about whether ATS systems fairly evaluate people for jobs. In 2024, the American Civil Liberties Union, or ACLU, filed a complaint against the hiring technology company Aon because some of its assessments were promoted as “bias-free” even though discrimination concerns still existed. [11]

Companies have also taken action after discovering problems in their own hiring systems. Amazon, for example, stopped developing an AI recruiting tool after learning that it favored resumes associated with men over women. [12] Overall, these efforts show that automated hiring technology needs oversight and regulation and should not automatically be treated as fair.

Solutions

Even though some laws and policies are already in place, much more could be done to make Applicant Tracking Systems and other automated hiring tools fairer. One possible solution would be to remove these tools and return to manual review. Although this may sound unconventional, having an actual recruiter review a resume could give a qualified candidate a better chance of being considered than a decision made by an algorithm or other tool.

Automated systems may reject applicants because they are missing certain keywords or because their resumes do not match the system’s criteria for the job. A human recruiter could look beyond those factors and consider a person’s experience, skills, and potential for the position.

However, completely removing these tools may not be realistic for larger companies that receive thousands of applications for a single position. Human recruiters can also have their own biases, so manual review by itself would not completely solve the problem. Companies could use these systems to help organize applications while leaving important decisions, such as rejecting or advancing a candidate, to trained recruiters.

Another solution would be for companies to test their systems for bias regularly by checking which groups are rejected at higher rates based on race, gender, age, disability, or other protected characteristics. New York City has already taken this approach by requiring certain automated employment decision tools to undergo bias audits. [9] Regular testing could help companies catch problems early instead of waiting until discrimination complaints or lawsuits occur.

Using both human review and regular bias testing would allow companies to keep some of the efficiency of automated hiring while making the process fairer and more accountable.
