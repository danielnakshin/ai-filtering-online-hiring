AI filtering and hiring

AI Filtering on Job Sites

This website looks at AI filtering on job sites such as Workday and Greenhouse. The goal is to understand whether people are being rejected from jobs because of discriminatory factors such as race, gender, age, and other information connected to their applications.

What is the problem?

An Applicant Tracking System, or ATS, provides the structure that allows recruitment to work at a large scale. It centralizes candidate information, job postings, applications, and hiring workflows into one system. [1]

Applicant tracking systems are often marketed as neutral, but technology is not neutral. A system can be affected by the data it was trained on and may reject qualified candidates before a human being ever sees their application. This can cause employers to overlook people who could bring real value to an organization simply because they did not use the “right” keywords on their resume. [3]

History of applicant tracking systems

The first applicant tracking systems were introduced in the 1970s. They were designed to manage applications, and most of the work was still done by people. During the 1980s, these systems began using more advanced filtering, which shortened the time recruiters needed to sort and analyze large numbers of resumes. As the internet grew, applicant tracking systems became connected with online jobs and made it faster for recruiters to sort through applications. [1][2]

Today’s applicant tracking systems are much more advanced. They can increase productivity, identify possible candidates, and automate repetitive tasks. These abilities make hiring faster, but they also allow the technology to have more influence over who gets considered for a job.

Who is affected?

Qualified applicants can be rejected when a system relies on flawed or biased data. Information connected to resume gaps, race, gender, ZIP codes, age, and other parts of an application can affect how a person is screened. The result can be discrimination against marginalized groups and a wider equality gap. [3]

This is also a risk for employers. In a competitive job market, a company can lose possible long-term employees because an applicant did not use the keywords that the system expected. The person may be rejected even though they are qualified for the role.

One example involved iTutorGroup. The company agreed to pay $365,000 to settle a lawsuit brought by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The EEOC said the company’s software automatically rejected women who were 55 or older and men who were 60 or older. [4]

What has been done so far?

Government agencies have become more involved as people have learned more about bias in applicant tracking systems and other automated hiring tools. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission says an employer can still be held responsible for discrimination when an algorithm or AI system is involved in a hiring decision. A company cannot avoid responsibility just because the decision came from a tool. [5]

The U.S. Department of Justice and the EEOC have also warned that automated hiring tools can affect people with disabilities. Some tools may measure traits or behaviors that are not connected to a person’s ability to do the job. This can cause a qualified applicant to be rejected for something that does not matter to the position. [6]

Some states and cities have created laws for automated hiring. New York City’s Local Law 144 requires certain automated employment decision tools to receive a bias audit, and employers must tell applicants when one of these tools will be used. Illinois also requires employers using AI to analyze video interviews to notify applicants, explain how the technology works, and receive consent before using it. [7][8]

Advocacy groups and companies have responded too. In 2024, the American Civil Liberties Union filed a complaint against the hiring technology company Aon because some assessments were promoted as “bias-free” even though discrimination concerns remained. Amazon also stopped developing an AI recruiting tool after finding that it favored resumes associated with men over women. [9][10]

These actions show that automated hiring technology needs oversight and should not automatically be treated as fair.

Solutions

One possible solution would be to remove automated screening tools and return to manual review. A recruiter may be able to look past missing keywords and consider a person’s experience, skills, and potential. This could give qualified applicants a better chance than a system that rejects resumes because they do not match its expected wording.

Completely removing these tools may not be realistic for large companies that receive thousands of applications for one position. Human recruiters can also have their own biases, so manual review by itself would not completely solve the problem.

A more balanced solution would be to use applicant tracking systems to organize applications while leaving important decisions, such as rejecting or advancing a candidate, to trained recruiters. Companies could also test their systems regularly and check whether people are being rejected at different rates based on race, gender, age, disability, or other protected characteristics. New York City already requires bias audits for certain automated employment decision tools. [7]

Using both human review and regular bias testing would let companies keep some of the speed of automated hiring while making the process fairer and more accountable.
